How to use this. Copy the funding rate straight from the exchange's contract details — it is quoted per settlement, usually every 8 hours on Binance or 4 hours on most altcoin pairs, so check the interval before you trust the yearly number. Multiply the per-cycle cost by how many cycles you expect to hold. A position held for six hours pays once, not six times. Negative rates mean shorts pay longs; if the sign flips against your direction, the same number becomes income instead of cost. Before entering any altcoin trade, run the annualised figure against your expected holding period — anything above 100% annualised is a crowded trade, and crowded trades exit through a narrow door.

How to use this. Enter the price you actually filled at, not the price you wish you had. The liquidation estimate is deliberately conservative — it ignores maintenance margin tiers and funding, so treat it as the earliest point you could be forced out. The practical number is not liquidation, it is where you plan to exit: if your stop sits more than a third of the way to the liquidation price, the position is too large for this market's volatility. For altcoins, halve the leverage you would use on a major pair. The quantity field is the amount of the coin you are actually carrying, which is what you paste into the exchange order form when you want to close a specific fraction of the position.

How to use this. Put the entry, the stop, and the target where you genuinely expect to exit — not where the chart looks prettiest. The raw ratio is the number most traders quote; the two lines underneath are the ones that matter, because fees are charged on both entry and exit and they quietly eat every trade that barely works. A setup that shows 1.8:1 raw is often 1.5:1 after fees, and at a 40% hit rate 1.5:1 barely breaks even. The breakeven price tells you how far price must travel just to undo the round trip. If your target is inside that band, you are not trading a setup, you are paying the exchange for entertainment.
