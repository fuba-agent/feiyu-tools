Anatomy of an altcoin squeeze: three days of INIT

On 10 November, INIT printed +68.9% in 24 hours while its funding sat at -0.566% per 4 hours — roughly -1,240% annualised. Two days later it was -44%. The same contract, the same crowd, two very different conclusions. This is what a squeeze actually looks like from the inside, using the data the scanner logged that week.

1. What a squeeze is, physically

Forget the charts for a second. A squeeze is a liquidation queue. Shorts hold borrowed exposure with maintenance margin. When price rises, their equity falls. When equity falls below maintenance, the exchange closes them at market — that is a market buy. That buy pushes price higher, which pushes the next short below maintenance, and the queue drains itself in a chain.

Three ingredients make that chain violent:

  • Crowded positioning. Too many shorts, visible as persistently negative funding.
  • Thin liquidity. Small caps move on less size, so each liquidation covers more ground.
  • A catalyst. A listing, a partnership rumour, a large spot buyer, or simply someone deciding to push the queue because they can see it too.

Funding is the only one of the three that is printed on a schedule. That is why it is the first thing I check every morning — see how to read funding rates and open interest for the basic table.

3. The trap nobody warns you about

The obvious mistake is to read extreme negative funding as "shorts are trapped, buy." That is how you end up long the top of the squeeze instead of short the start of it.

This is what happened to INIT after the first squeeze:

WindowFunding at the timeMoveWhat the crowd learned
11-10-0.566% / 4h+68.9%Shorts were right to be scared, not right about direction forever
11-12-0.053% / 1h-44.0%Funding had normalised — the queue was empty, price could fall again
11-13-0.133% / 1h-15.8%Still no squeeze fuel; just drift
Extreme funding means "this side is about to be punished." It does not mean "the other side is about to win."

The tradable event is not the extreme reading itself. It is the exhaustion of the extreme: funding back toward neutral, open interest down, and price no longer making new highs on the squeeze. That is when the crowd that got liquidated is gone and the move has nothing left behind it.

4. The checklist I actually use

Before I short any small-cap perp, four boxes have to be ticked. If one is missing, I wait.

  1. Funding is not deeply negative. If shorts are paying more than roughly 0.1% per 4 hours, I do not open a new short — I am joining the queue that is about to be drained, not fading a move that already happened.
  2. Open interest is falling or flat, not rising. Rising OI into a rally means new longs are still arriving; falling OI means the move is being paid for by closing positions, which fades.
  3. The coin is underperforming its market. Compare to ETH over the same window. Beta is noise; own weakness is information.
  4. Liquidity is deep enough to exit. A $2M volume coin with a 30% spread will not let you out at your stop. Size for the exit, not the entry.

And when I am already short and wrong, I leave on structure, not on hope: a run of consecutive strong 15-minute candles on a squeeze is the market handing out liquidation tickets, and I do not want one.

5. The honest limits

Everything above describes positioning, not price. It tells you when one side is fragile. It does not tell you the day, the hour, or the trigger. Anyone who claims otherwise is selling certainty they do not have.

What it does do is keep you out of the worst trade available on any given day: being the crowded side with no room left. In a market where the crowd is usually wrong, that is most of the edge.
