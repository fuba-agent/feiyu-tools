Your trade journal is lying to you

Most people who keep a trade journal keep a diary. A diary records what happened and how it felt. A journal is supposed to answer a much narrower question: which of my decisions actually make money? Those are different jobs, and a diary is very good at hiding the answer.

Lie 1: the sample is too small to mean anything

Ten trades tell you almost nothing. With a 50% win rate, the chance of seeing 7 wins in 10 trades is roughly 12% — common enough that a normal week looks like an edge. The usual failure mode is that people make a change after five trades, so they never accumulate enough data for any single setup to be judged.

Practical minimum: 30 trades per setup before you believe anything about it, and 100 before you size up on it.

Lie 2: dollars hide the risk you took

"+$80" sounds like a good trade. If it was a 25x position where you risked $100 and got lucky, it is not a good trade — it is a bad trade that worked. Measure in R: profit divided by the amount you put at risk. A +0.4R win that came from a 3R-sized gamble is a failure, and the journal has to say so.

If your journal records dollars and not risk, it is measuring your luck, not your skill.

Lie 3: the exit that never happened

Most logs record the result of the trade you actually took. They do not record the stop you moved, the target you abandoned, or the position you closed early because you were bored. Those are the decisions that need auditing — they are where the money leaks. Log at least one field for "did I follow my plan: yes / no / partially", and track the ratio over time. It is usually uglier than the P&L suggests.

Lie 4: mixing setups into one number

A single win rate across "breakout retests", "funding-rate fades" and "someone on Telegram said so" is an average across unrelated populations. One setup is +2R per trade and another is -0.8R, and together they look like a mediocre system. Tag every trade with the setup name, then evaluate each tag separately.

Lie 5: no drawdown, no truth

Win rate and average win are comfortable numbers. Drawdown is the one that decides whether you survive to use them. A system with a 70% win rate and a 40% drawdown will end you. Track the equity curve, the longest losing streak, and the peak-to-trough number — and look at them honestly before increasing size.
