Why the scanner exists

I trade a small book, mostly short altcoin perpetuals. The edge I actually have is not prediction — it is attention. If I can look at 800 contracts in a way that takes thirty seconds instead of ninety minutes, I make fewer lazy decisions and I stop opening positions simply because a chart was already on my screen.

The scanner does one thing: it narrows the board to the handful of contracts behaving abnormally right now — funding far from normal, a 24-hour move that does not match the rest of the market, a five-minute spike, open interest jumping while price does not. It does not rank them, score them, or tell you which way to trade. That part is still yours, and it should be.

Why the journal exists

I kept repeating the same three mistakes for years, and my P&L was too coarse to show them. A journal that only records "win" and "loss" cannot tell you whether you lose money because of entries or because you move stops. So the template computes the things that actually change behaviour: R-multiples instead of dollars, payoff ratio next to win rate, drawdown, expectancy by setup, and the gap between your planned stop and the stop you actually honoured.

It is an Excel file because I do not want to log into anything, and because you should own the sheet you keep your own history in.

How I trade — the short version

  • Fixed risk per trade, decided before the entry, never adjusted because a trade is going badly. The most expensive habit in this market is widening a stop.
  • Limit orders only. If the price is not where I planned, I am not in.
  • Small book, one position at a time. Correlated altcoins are one bet wearing five tickers.
  • No signals, no calls, no group. Everything I sell is software that reads public data. Nothing is a recommendation.

If you want the longer version, it is scattered through the notes: how to read funding rates and open interest, what a squeeze looks like from the inside, and the four numbers most trade journals never compute.
